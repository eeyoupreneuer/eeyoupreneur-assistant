INTRODUCTION
The Cree Nation of Nemaska is one of the eleven communities that make up Eeyou
Istchee, or James Bay Cree territory. With a population of approximately 1,000 people,
Nemaska is an inland community located on the shore of Lake Champion, in the heart
of Eeyou Istchee. It hosts the head office of the Grand Council of the Crees and the
Cree Nation Government.
Economic development services in Nemaska fall under the responsibility of the
Department of Land and Sustainable Development. The community’s last economic
development strategic plan was developed in 2021 but is incomplete. The
Department wishes to develop a new strategic plan that approaches economic
development from a holistic perspective and that will provide a solid roadmap for
structuring economic development activities for the years to come.
The strategic planning process took place between June 2024 and October 2025 and
was structured in three phases, as presented below.

Phase 1: Kick-off and Analysis
•Kick-off meeting with the Steering Committee
•Document review
•Individual interviews

Phase 2: Consultations and Strategic Workshops
•Focus groups with land users, entrepreneurs and Nemaska Development
Corporation
•Two workshops with a strategic planning committee of representatives from
local government, Nemaska Development Corporation, Land and Sustainable
Development department and the Economic Development team

Phase 3: Drafting the Strategic Plan
•Preliminary verison of the strategic plan
•Working meeting with members of the Economic Development team
•Presentation and validation of the strategic plan with the strategic planning
committee

STRATEGIC FRAMEWORK
The strategic framework represents the foundation of the strategic plan. Its
components help us understand the current state of affairs and make it possible
prioritize projects and develop an action plan. Nemaska’s strategic framework
includes its strategic assets, economic development challenges and its economic
development vision.

Strategic Assets
Strategic assets represent tangible and intangible elements that create value for the
community. They are elements that can be leveraged as current or potential sources
of development. The various focus groups and workshops held during the strategic
planning process led to the identification of Nemaska’s key strategic assets.

Nemaska’s Strategic Assets:
❖ Quality of the built environment: new buildings, diverse community
infrastructure
❖ Peaceful nature of the community: quiet, cleanliness
❖ Community spirit: collective pride in the community, sense of being a big family,
welcoming nature of the community
❖ Scenery and landscapes: Lake Champion, preservation of trees within in the
community, landscaping, boardwalks, etc.
❖ Road connections to Chibougamau and Matagami
❖ Presence of resources on the territory: mining, forestry, hydro
❖ Treaties and agreements: JBNQA, IBAs, etc.
❖ Old Nemaska site
❖ Diversity of existing programs for entrepreneurs, youth, etc.
A strategic asset may be underexploited or unexploited, representing untapped
potential that the community may be able to better draw on for its development. The
following underexploited and unexploited assets were identified during the strategic
planning process:
❖ Geographical location: heart of Eeyou Istchee, proximity to resource
development projects
❖ A young and growing population
❖ The land / territory: non-timber forest products, tourism etc.*
❖ Cree knowledge and culture: land-based skills and traditional activities*

Economic Development Challenges
Defining key challenges is an essential part of the strategic planning process, as it
enables the identification of factors that hinder the community’s development. The
various consultations and workshops conducted in Nemaska allowed us to identify
these economic development challenges, presented below in six key categories.
ACCESS CHALLENGES
• Limited market size (small population, competition for customers)

• Geographical isolation (cost of supplies, transportation)
INFRASTRUCTURE CHALLENGES
• Lack of space and infrastructure for entrepreneurs (availability,
spaces adapted to needs)
• Insufficient housing (ex. for employees)
FINANCIAL CHALLENGES
• Competition for employees by small businesses against better paying
jobs (ex. administration)

• High construction costs (labour and materials)
• Risk-averse mindset (ex. preference for holding capital over investing)
LEGAL CHALLENGES
• Legal framework that limits investment possibilities (JBNQA and Indian
Act legacy)

• Jurisdictional issues on Category II and III lands (overlapping
responsibilities, lack of control over Category III)
CAPACITY CHALLENGES
• Lack of administrative skills among entrepreneurs (ex. accounting,
business registration, taxes, software)
• Limited knowledge of French leading to language barriers for tasks
such as business registration
• Misunderstanding of entrepreneurship (perception that
entrepreneurs have it easy and are rich)

• Understanding the needs of regional and external markets
COMMUNICATIONAL CHALLENGES
• Communication and coordination between different departments and
projects within the community
• Communication and marketing of existing economic development
initiatives

Strategic Projects
Numerous projects were identified during the consultations and strategic workshops. These projects were then prioritized to
narrow down the list to the most strategic projects that provide a concrete response to the community’s economic development
challenges and contribute to achieving the economic development vision. The following pages present the projects associated
with each strategic pillar, including the entities responsible for each project and a timeline for project completion.
The projects are categorized according to the legend presented below. This categorization ensures a balance between the most
ambitious projects and those that are more operational in nature.
SI – Strategic Initiatives
These projects are highly strategic and have the potential to generate significant and
lasting impact. These projects will require a major investment of financial and human
resources, often throughout the lifespan of the strategic plan. Due to the investments
required, a strategic plan typically includes one to three strategic initiatives.

SI

VAP – Value-added Projects
These are high-value projects that require more effort than daily operations. These
projects generate short- or medium-term impacts and require a moderate
investment of financial and human resources, often over one or two years. VAPs make
up the middle of the pyramid, as they require fewer resources that strategic initiatives
but a greater investment than core operations.

VAP

CO – Core Operations
These projects are part of an organization’s daily routine and are carried out in order
for the organization to fulfill its mission. Even if the project is new, it is in continuity
with the organization’s existing initiatives. While an organization will have many core
operations, a strategic plan typically only includes those that are seen as strategic for
addressing key challenges and meeting the organization’s strategic vision.
